# Fictional tasks and calibration challenges

**Task version:** HALA-KIT-0.1.1 · English draft  
All entities, sources and numbers below are fictional. Source cards are task materials, not external references. No internet lookup is necessary. Neither task is validated or matched in difficulty.

## Task L — Did the quiet programme improve reading?

A town library announces: “Our quiet reading programme improved comprehension.”

### Source cards

**L1 · Programme register.** Twenty adults applied voluntarily for the programme. Their mean reading score was 60 before and 75 afterwards. Twenty adults who did not apply had mean scores of 60 and 65. Everyone took the same forms at each occasion; all 40 completed both tests.

**L2 · Scheduling note.** Participants chose whether to apply. The programme included quiet seating, extra practice sheets and longer library opening hours. Allocation was not random. Staff do not know whether groups differed in motivation, outside practice or other characteristics.

**L3 · Announcement.** The library attributes the larger score increase to the quiet environment. It does not publish individual scores, score variability or evidence that the pre/post test forms were comparable.

### Your response

In a short paragraph or bullets:

1. What do these records support, and what remains uncertain?
2. Use at least two relevant details from the cards.
3. Give a plausible alternative explanation.
4. Propose a comparison or observation that would help distinguish explanations, and say what result would change your conclusion.

No technical vocabulary or statistical calculation is required. A concise supported answer can receive the strongest anchor. “More research is needed” by itself is insufficient.

### Optional synthetic facilitation rehearsal

B can explain observational versus randomized comparisons, using L only. C can ask the learner to state a conclusion, consider an alternative and specify discriminating evidence, using L only. Both must use the same source cards and accurate content. Preserve prompts, turn order and assistance provided.

These are design suggestions, not finalized interventions. No live model is needed: synthetic dialogues are enough for this version. Facilitators must not see or rehearse future protected assessment items.

## Task I — Did the coating reduce electricity use?

**Intended rehearsal condition:** independently written response, without AI, Task L answers or rubric. The source cards remain available. Actual compliance is not established merely by labeling a response independent.

An office manager announces: “Reflective roof coating reduced summer electricity consumption.”

### Source cards

**I1 · Meter summary.** Twelve building owners voluntarily bought the coating. Their average summer electricity use fell from 100 to 80 units. Twelve owners without the coating went from 100 to 90 units. All 24 buildings have records for both summers; unit definitions and meter procedures were unchanged.

**I2 · Maintenance record.** Buyers were not assigned randomly. Eight of the twelve coated buildings also replaced their air-conditioning equipment. Weather was milder in the second summer for both groups. Floor area, occupancy and cooling habits were not fully recorded.

**I3 · Seller statement.** The seller attributes the larger reduction to the coating alone. No individual building records, variability estimates or equipment-adjusted comparisons are provided.

### Your response

Use the same four questions as Task L. Explain the direction of the evidence in ordinary language; exact arithmetic is optional.

### Conditions to record in a future approved rehearsal

Task/rubric version, response condition, time allowed and spent, assistance availability, actual assistance if known, deviations and missing fields. Do not infer authorship from stylistic resemblance.

This task can rehearse applying an idea to another setting. It does not establish form equivalence, independent learning gain, delayed retention or broad transfer. Human pilots would require the existing review and ethics gates.


## Challenge R — When causal confidence is justified

**Role:** synthetic positive control for the rubric, not an adopted learning-study item.

**R1 · Design card.** Four hundred consenting adults were randomly assigned, with concealed allocation, to quiet or usual seating (200 each). Materials, practice time and opening hours were matched. Scorers were blind to seating assignment. All participants completed the reviewed, comparable test forms. No seating crossover or implementation deviation is recorded.

**R2 · Results card.** Improvement was on average 4 points greater under quiet seating. For this fictional exercise, the reported uncertainty range is 1 to 7 points: it supports a positive average effect in this study, with uncertainty about its size. Accept the card's stipulated analysis; no interval calculation is required.

**R3 · Scope card.** The study concerns one adult population, one library setting and short-term performance. It does not assess other settings or delayed retention.

**Question:** what conclusion is justified, what remains uncertain, and what follow-up result would change how broadly you apply it? Do not invent a flaw absent from the cards. It is reasonable to ask about implementation, but an unsupported suspicion is not evidence that the experiment failed.

## Challenge U — When a near-zero estimate is inconclusive

**Role:** synthetic uncertainty control; a separate fictional study, not another analysis of R.

**U1 · Design card.** The design and assessment conditions follow R, but only 40 adults were randomized (20 per condition), all completing the assessment.

**U2 · Results card.** The estimated average difference is zero. The stipulated uncertainty range spans a 6-point disadvantage to a 6-point advantage. A 3-point difference was specified as practically important before analysis. No equivalence conclusion is warranted from this range.

**Question:** what can we conclude, and what evidence would distinguish a negligible effect from an imprecise estimate?

These cards give uncertainty in ordinary language. They do not teach interval-estimation methods or constitute real statistical output. R and U are rubric challenges; they are not equated with L/I or authorized additions to P01's outcome battery.
